Extracting attributes

The fastest way to improve your interview performance is to stop preparing for “questions” and start preparing for “themes”. Public hospital interview questions often change wording from panel to panel, but the underlying themes are usually consistent.

This page is a step-by-step method to read a position description and pull out the attributes that are important for the position and likely to be assessed.

Output 1: Role attribute list

  • Explicit criteria (what the document literally tells you)
  • Explicit values (often the organisation’s stated values)
  • Implicit values and signals (what the responsibilities reveal)
  • Your own “extra” desirable attributes for the role

Output 2: Your best attributes list

  • Your strongest skills and strengths (separate from the role)
  • Evidence you can use to prove them
  • What you want to be remembered for
Important: we keep these two lists separate on purpose. Later pages will show how to combine them into a flexible set of examples and answers.

What does “attributes” mean here?

When people say “key attributes”, they usually mean a mix of:

  • Capability (what you can do)
  • Behaviour (how you work with others)
  • Judgement (how you think and manage risk)
  • Values (what you focus on when trade-offs are required)
  • Impact (what changes because you are there)

A common mistake is preparing only for tasks (what you have done) and not preparing to show the panel how you think and behave.

Step 1: Extracting key attributes from the role description

Start simple: read the job ad and role description carefully. Look for any documents that describe the team, the service, or the work area. If you can, phone the contact person or speak to someone who works in the area to get more context. The more you understand about the role, the better you can extract the right attributes.

Tip: over time you get better at spotting recurring themes, which also helps you ask better questions when you speak to the team.

1A. Find the explicit criteria

Some role descriptions are very direct. They literally tell you what a “good applicant” can demonstrate. Look for sections with headings like:

  • “Are you the right person for the job?”
  • “The ideal applicant will demonstrate…”
  • “Selection criteria” or “What we are looking for”

In many documents, the phrasing is very consistent. It often looks like: category heading plus a sentence starting with “Demonstrated ability…” or “Demonstrated high level…”.

1B. Find the explicit values

Some role descriptions also include a clear statement of organisational values. If the values are listed, treat them as assessable behaviour, not just words on a poster. In practical terms, values often influence what the panel considers “good judgement”, “good communication”, and “a good team fit”.

1C. Extract the implicit values and signals

Even when values are not written as a neat list, they are still in the document. They show up through responsibilities and expectations, for example:

  • how much independence is expected
  • how complex the workload is
  • how much teamwork, influence, and negotiation is required
  • how strongly safety, documentation, and follow-through are emphasised
  • what kinds of people the role interacts with (patients, carers, community teams, inpatient teams)
Important reality check: the criteria and values written in the role description are not always exactly what the panel cares about most. Some role descriptions are generic and used for multiple vacancies. That is why it is worth asking targeted questions and also thinking for yourself about what will actually be necessary or desirable for success in the role.

Doing that extra thinking is useful for two reasons:

  • It helps you prepare for what the panel probably cares about, even if it is not written perfectly.
  • It can reveal one or two “extra” desirable attributes that the panel may not have explicitly listed, which helps you stand out and shows you understand the role properly.
Quick note about interview questions: sometimes the question will name the attribute (for example, “Tell us about a time you managed conflict”). Other times it will not, and you will need to recognise what value or attribute is being tested. We will deal with question interpretation and mapping later.

Worked example: deidentified senior pharmacist role description

This example is based on your uploaded role description, but written in deidentified, plain-language form.

What the role description makes explicit

In this case, the document is quite direct. It contains an “are you the right person” section that lists headings and “demonstrated ability” statements. That is essentially your first draft list of assessable themes.

How it is written in the document What it usually means as an attribute What to listen for in questions
Category headings (for example, clinical/technical, teamwork, leadership, improvement) with “Demonstrated high level…” or “Demonstrated ability…” statements. The panel is likely assessing capability and behaviour across those buckets. It is not just “what you know”, it is how you work and how you make decisions. Questions may be behaviour-based (“tell us about a time”), scenario-based (“what would you do if”), or judgement-based (“how would you prioritise”).
Explicit mention of communication, negotiation, and conflict resolution. You can communicate clearly, influence decisions, and handle disagreement without becoming defensive or vague. Listen for prompts about difficult conversations, managing expectations, or disagreement between colleagues.
Explicit mention of rapid prioritisation in a complex environment. You can triage, stay calm, and keep high-risk work safe even when interrupted or under time pressure. Listen for prompts about busy shifts, multiple competing demands, interruptions, and making trade-offs.
Explicit mention of supervision, training, and support of less experienced staff or students. You can lift the standard of work around you, not just do your own tasks. You can explain, coach, and give feedback respectfully. Listen for prompts about teaching, mentoring, supporting new starters, or handling performance issues kindly but clearly.
Explicit mention of initiating, planning, and evaluating improvement activities. You can notice recurring problems, propose practical changes, and follow through. Not just ideas, but implementation and evaluation. Listen for prompts about improving processes, audit, quality activities, or “tell us about a change you led”.

Explicit values

The document also includes a set of organisational values and a section on inclusion. When those are listed, take them seriously. You do not need to recite them, but your examples should sound consistent with them.

How values usually show up in interviews

  • how you treat people when stressed
  • how you handle mistakes, risk, and accountability
  • how you speak about patients and colleagues
  • how you respond to people with different backgrounds or needs

When values are not explicit

Some role descriptions do not list values clearly. In those cases, you extract them from the responsibilities and from the organisation’s public messaging.

This is why it helps to skim the organisation’s “values” page and any “about us” section before the interview.

Implicit values and “hidden” attributes in this example

This role description also contains a lot of implicit signals. For example, it strongly hints that the role needs someone who:

  • works in an evidence-led way (not just habit-led)
  • can operate with a high degree of independence, but still escalates early when unsure
  • can work across hospital and community settings and keep handovers safe
  • can build relationships beyond the immediate team (for example, community organisations)
  • can produce accurate, high-quality written documentation consistently
  • keeps care patient-centred and compassionate, even when busy
How to use implicit attributes: add them to your list as “desirable attributes”. Even if they do not appear as a formal criterion, they help you prepare stronger, more tailored answers.

When the role description is generic

Sometimes the document is broad and could fit multiple vacancies. If that happens, do two things:

Ask specific questions

  • “What does success look like in the first 8 to 12 weeks?”
  • “Where does this role add the most value day-to-day?”
  • “What are the biggest pressure points for the team?”
  • “What would you like the new person to improve or stabilise?”

Add one or two “extra” desirable attributes

  • calm under frequent interruptions
  • excellent written follow-through
  • safe and confident escalation
  • practical problem-solving that reduces friction for others

These can become your edge if they are grounded in what the role actually involves.

Step 2: Extract your best attributes (separately from the role)

This section is not about matching yourself to the themes you identified above. This is about getting clear on your strongest attributes as a professional, with evidence.

Why do this separately? Because interviews are not only about ticking criteria. Your best attributes are often what makes you memorable, and what makes the panel feel confident you will do well in the team.

2A. Start with a strengths brainstorm (by category)

Write down anything that could genuinely be one of your strengths, then tighten it later:

  • Clinical expertise: complex cases, high-risk medicines, specialist knowledge
  • Education: teaching, coaching, building resources, explaining clearly
  • Service improvement: audits, fixing recurring problems, standardising processes
  • Research: projects, posters, publications, data work
  • Leadership: leading change, mentoring, stepping up when needed
  • Communication: influencing decisions, difficult conversations, written clarity
  • Organisation: prioritisation, structure, calm under pressure

2B. Choose your top 5 (only if you can prove them)

If you cannot attach evidence to it, it is not one of your best attributes yet. It can still be a development goal, but do not lead with it.

2C. Turn each attribute into a “proof line”

This prevents vague statements like “I’m a hard worker”. Your proof line should be one sentence that sounds like you, and points to evidence. 

ATTRIBUTE: ________________________________________

PROOF LINE (one sentence):
“You will see this in me because ________________________________.”

EXAMPLE TITLE (short):
________________________________________

OUTCOME (what changed?):
________________________________________
Reality check: If your best attributes have little overlap with what the role needs, that is not a failure. It is information. You can either fill gaps, choose a different role, or accept that you will need a very deliberate strategy.
