Extracting attributes from the role description, and from yourself

This is about getting crystal clear on two separate things: what the role is asking for, and what your strongest attributes actually are. You bring them together later when you map examples to likely question themes.

Role attributes Explicit criteria Implicit values Your strongest attributes

Quick definitions (so we are talking about the same thing)

Role attributes

The things the team needs from a person in the role. Some are written plainly (criteria), and others are hinted at through responsibilities, context, and organisational values.

  • Capability: what you can do safely and reliably
  • Behaviour: how you work with people
  • Judgement: how you make decisions and manage risk
  • Values: what you prioritise when trade-offs exist
  • Impact: what improves because you are there

Your best attributes

The things you do better than most people at your level, plus the evidence you can use to prove it. This is a separate exercise from the role description.

If your best attributes and the role’s needs barely overlap, that is useful information. It may not be the right role, or you may need a plan to address gaps.

Part A: Pull attributes out of a role description (without overthinking it)

Aim for a shortlist you can actually prepare for: usually 5 to 7 themes.

Pass 1

Circle the “purpose” and write it in plain language

Most role descriptions start with a short purpose statement. Do not memorise it. Translate it into one sentence in your own words.

One-sentence purpose template

This role exists to __________________________________________
by __________________________________________
with a strong focus on __________________________________________.

In the sample senior pharmacist role description, the purpose section describes delivering senior-level clinical services, contributing to service development and improvement, monitoring outcomes, and supporting less experienced staff. Pass 2

Pull out the explicit criteria (the document tells you what they will assess)

Some role descriptions spoon-feed you the assessment criteria. When you see a section like “Are you the right person for the job?”, treat it like a signpost. It is not subtle.

In the sample senior pharmacist role description, there is a section that basically says: “How we do things matters as much as what we do”, then lists criteria headings with “Demonstrated ability…” statements. [oai_citation:1‡MS03676879 - Role Description.pdf](sediment://file_00000000e3a072068befa10dc7f29512)

What “explicit criteria” looks like

  • “Demonstrated high level…” (knowledge, skill, expertise)
  • “Demonstrated ability to communicate…”
  • “Demonstrated ability to manage conflict…”
  • “Demonstrated ability to prioritise…” in a complex environment
  • “Demonstrated ability to supervise / support others…”
  • “Demonstrated ability to initiate and evaluate improvement…”

Those criteria headings and examples are shown directly in the sample role description. [oai_citation:2‡MS03676879 - Role Description.pdf](sediment://file_00000000e3a072068befa10dc7f29512)

How to convert criteria into themes

Do not copy their headings word-for-word. Convert them into simple themes you can prepare for:

  • Clinical expertise and judgement
  • Communication and influence
  • Prioritisation and workflow management
  • Supporting others (teaching, supervision)
  • Improvement mindset (making services better)

These map cleanly to the sample’s explicit criteria sections. [oai_citation:3‡MS03676879 - Role Description.pdf](sediment://file_00000000e3a072068befa10dc7f29512)

One caution: some role descriptions use generic criteria that could apply to lots of roles. That is where your own thinking matters, because the panel might care about things that are not written down clearly.

Pass 3

Pull out explicit values (the “how we behave here” section)

Many organisations publish a set of values. Even if you never say the value words out loud in an interview, the panel often responds well when your examples clearly match them.

In the sample role description, organisational values are listed directly (integrity, compassion, accountability, respect, engagement, excellence). [oai_citation:4‡MS03676879 - Role Description.pdf](sediment://file_00000000e3a072068befa10dc7f29512)

Values as behaviour

Not slogans. What do they look like?

  • Honest, transparent communication
  • Kindness under pressure
  • Owning mistakes and fixing systems
  • Respectful teamwork and boundaries

Equity and inclusion signals

Sometimes this is explicit too.

The sample role description includes a diversity and inclusion section, framing inclusive care and a workforce that reflects the community. [oai_citation:5‡MS03676879 - Role Description.pdf](sediment://file_00000000e3a072068befa10dc7f29512)

Your practical move

One line you can use in prep.

“I try to be someone people can rely on: clear, respectful, and accountable, especially when it is busy.”

Pass 4

Extract the implicit values (the stuff they never label as “values”)

This is where you get a genuine edge. The document often tells you what the team believes is “good work” through repeated phrases and responsibilities.

Examples of implicit signals in the sample senior pharmacist role

  • Evidence-based practice: repeated references to evidence-based care and clinical decision-making. [oai_citation:6‡MS03676879 - Role Description.pdf](sediment://file_00000000e3a072068befa10dc7f29512)
  • Team-first interprofessional work: repeated focus on functioning within an interprofessional team. [oai_citation:7‡MS03676879 - Role Description.pdf](sediment://file_00000000e3a072068befa10dc7f29512)
  • Patient-centred and compassionate care: described directly within the collaboration and engagement section. [oai_citation:8‡MS03676879 - Role Description.pdf](sediment://file_00000000e3a072068befa10dc7f29512)
  • Respect and fairness: explicitly stated as treating people fairly regardless of background. [oai_citation:9‡MS03676879 - Role Description.pdf](sediment://file_00000000e3a072068befa10dc7f29512)
  • Quality and safety habits: service quality standards and safe workplaces are referenced. [oai_citation:10‡MS03676879 - Role Description.pdf](sediment://file_00000000e3a072068befa10dc7f29512)

Turn “implicit signals” into desirable attributes

For each repeated idea, write the attribute as a person-level quality:

  • “Works in a way that is evidence-led, not habit-led.”
  • “Communicates clearly across different disciplines.”
  • “Keeps care compassionate and practical, even when busy.”
  • “Treats people fairly and adjusts communication to the person.”
  • “Notices risks early and acts before they become incidents.”

This matters because not every interview question will tell you the theme. Sometimes the panel expects you to recognise what they are really testing. We will cover question interpretation later, but the groundwork starts here.

You will notice that some of these “implicit” attributes overlap with the explicit criteria. That is normal. Repetition usually means importance.

Reality check

Role descriptions are helpful, but not always precise

Sometimes the document is detailed and specific. Other times it is generic and could be used for multiple vacancies. Either way, it is worth doing two things:

1) Ask targeted questions

This helps you understand the real priorities.

  • “What does a strong first 3 months look like in this role?”
  • “What are the busiest pressure points in a typical week?”
  • “What behaviours make someone a great fit for this team?”
  • “What would you like the person in this role to improve or stabilise?”

2) Add one or two ‘extra’ desirable attributes

This is where you can bring a unique perspective, as long as it is grounded in what the role actually involves.

  • Being calm and structured when plans change quickly
  • Practical problem-solving and removing friction for the team
  • Strong written documentation habits
  • Confident but safe escalation when uncertain

The sample role description explicitly values high quality documentation and clear written communication. [oai_citation:11‡MS03676879 - Role Description.pdf](sediment://file_00000000e3a072068befa10dc7f29512)

Done well, this gives you a double win: you understand the role better, and you can show the panel you have thought about success in the job beyond the generic wording.

Part B: Identify your best attributes (separately from the role description)

This is not “match yourself to the job”. This is: what are you genuinely strongest at, with evidence?

Think of this as building your personal headline. Later, you will decide how to position that headline for the role. For now, keep it honest and separate.

Step 1: Brain-dump your strengths by category

Aim for quantity first, then quality.

  • Clinical expertise: complex cases, high-risk medicines, deprescribing, geriatrics, antimicrobial stewardship, and so on
  • Education: teaching juniors, patient education, creating resources
  • Service improvement: audits, reducing errors, improving workflow, standardising processes
  • Research: projects, data, posters, publication involvement
  • Leadership: leading a change, acting roles, mentoring
  • Communication: difficult conversations, clear written notes, influencing decisions
  • Organisation: prioritisation, time management, calm under interruptions

Do not worry yet whether the role description asked for each of these. This is about you at your best.

Step 3: Turn each attribute into a “proof line”

This keeps you from sounding generic in interviews. Write one sentence per attribute:

  • “You will see this in me because…”
  • “A recent example is…”
  • “The outcome was…”
Attribute: ________________________________
Proof line:

Example title: _____________________________

Step 4: Check for mismatch (without panicking)

You are allowed to be strong in areas that the role does not care about. The question is what you do with that information.

  • If there is strong overlap, brilliant. You can lean into it.
  • If there is partial overlap, you choose which strengths to foreground.
  • If there is minimal overlap, you either fill gaps quickly or consider whether this role fits.

Treat “mismatch” as a planning tool, not a judgement. You can still perform well if you prepare deliberately.
