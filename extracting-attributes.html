Extracting attributes

The fastest way to improve your interview performance is to stop preparing for “questions” and start preparing for “themes”. Public hospital interview questions often change wording from panel to panel, but the underlying themes are usually consistent.

Output 1: Role attribute list

  • Explicit criteria (what the role description tells you)
  • Explicit values (often the organisation’s stated values)
  • Implicit values and signals
  • Your own “extra” desirable attributes for the role

Output 2: Your best attributes list

  • Your strongest skills and strengths (separate from the role)
Keep these two lists separate on purpose. Later pages will show how to combine them into a flexible set of examples and answers.

What does “attributes” mean here?

When people say “key attributes”, they usually mean a mix of:

  • Capability (what you can do)
  • Behaviour (how you work with others)
  • Judgement (how you think)
  • Values (what you focus on when trade-offs are required)
  • Impact (what changes because you are there)

A common mistake is preparing only for tasks (what you have done) and not preparing to show the panel how you think and behave.

Step 1: Extracting key attributes from the role description

Read the job ad and role description carefully. Look for any documents that describe the team, the service, or the work area. If you can, phone the contact person or speak to someone who works in the area to get more context. The more you understand about the role, the better you can extract the right attributes.

Over time you get better at spotting themes, which also helps you ask better questions when you speak to the team.

1A. Find the explicit criteria

Some role descriptions are very direct. They tell you what a “good applicant” can demonstrate. Look for sections with headings like:

  • “Are you the right person for the job?”
  • “The ideal applicant will demonstrate…”
  • “Selection criteria” or “What we are looking for”

In many documents, the phrasing is very consistent. It often looks like: category heading plus a sentence starting with “Demonstrated ability…” or “Demonstrated high level…”.

1B. Find the explicit values

Some role descriptions also include a clear statement of organisational values. If the values are listed, treat them as assessable behaviour. In practical terms, values often influence what the panel considers “good judgement”, “good communication”, and “a good team fit”, etc.

1C. Extract implicit values

When attributes and values are not written explicitly, they may show up through responsibilities and expectations, for example:

  • how much independence is expected
  • how complex the workload is
  • how much teamwork, influence, and negotiation is required
  • how strongly safety, documentation, and follow-through are emphasised
  • what kinds of people the role interacts with (patients, carers, community teams, inpatient teams)
N.B. the criteria and values written in the role description are not always exactly what the panel cares about most. Some role descriptions are generic and used for multiple vacancies. That is why it is worth asking targeted questions and also thinking for yourself about what will actually be necessary or desirable for success in the role.

Doing that extra thinking is useful for two reasons:

  • It helps you prepare for what the panel probably cares about, even if it is not written explicitly.
  • It can reveal one or two “extra” desirable attributes that the panel may not have explicitly listed, which helps you stand out and shows you understand the role properly.
Note about interview questions: sometimes the question will name the attribute (for example, “COMMUNICATION: Tell us about a time you managed conflict”). Other times it will not, and you will need to recognise what value or attribute is being tested.

Worked example: deidentified senior pharmacist role description

Below are excerpts from a real role description. The identifying details are removed.

How to use this: open each excerpt first and quickly jot down the themes you notice. Then read the notes underneath each excerpt to see how someone else might extract attributes from the same text.

Part 1: Explicit criteria (these are written as a scoring rubric)

(Click to open) Excerpt: “Are you the right person for the job?” (explicit criteria)

How we do things is as important as what we do, therefore within the context of the responsibilities described under “Your key responsibilities”, the ideal applicant will be someone who can demonstrate the following:

  • Clinical / Technical: Demonstrated high level of knowledge, expertise and skill in delivering senior clinical services to patients within a virtual ward and community-based model.
  • Collaboration, Teamwork & Consumer Engagement: Demonstrated ability to communicate and participate effectively in multidisciplinary and interdisciplinary teams, provide high level clinical advice to supervisors and stakeholders, and adequately address and manage conflict.
  • Individual and Work Unit Management: Demonstrated ability to provide clinical services of a complex nature including rapid prioritisation, management and discharge planning of patients in a complex clinical environment.
  • Leadership: Demonstrated ability to provide supervision, training and support to less experienced practitioners and students.
  • Service Improvement: Demonstrated ability to initiate, plan and evaluate local service improvement initiatives.

What to notice about the way this is written:

  • It gives you headings and it tells you exactly what they want you to “demonstrate”.
  • The phrase “demonstrated ability” is basically telling you: bring evidence, not just opinions.
  • The line “how we do things is as important as what we do” is a clue that behaviour and values matter, not only technical competence.

How to convert this into an attribute: keep the same five headings, but rewrite each one in plain language. This is your first draft “theme list”.

Explicit criterion heading Plain-language theme What it includes (the nuance)
Clinical / Technical Clinical experience and expertise Evidence-led decisions, safe practice, comfort with complexity, and knowing limitations.
Collaboration, Teamwork & Consumer Engagement Communication and teamwork Working across disciplines, giving advice confidently, negotiating, and managing conflict without becoming vague or defensive.
Individual and Work Unit Management Working independently, prioritisation, and organisation Experience in independent or autonomous role, values, staying structured when things are busy.
Leadership Supporting, supervising, or mentoring others Teaching, supervision, consistent standards, calm guidance, and giving feedback respectfully.
Service Improvement QUM, DUE, research, patient safety Noticing recurring issues, proposing fixes, and following through (including evaluating whether it worked).
Key point: even when a role description gives you a neat criteria list, it is still not guaranteed that this is exactly what the panel cares about most. Sometimes the criteria are broad, generic, or copied from a template.

Part 2: Explicit values (these are the ones listed under headings)

(Click to open) Excerpt: “Our values” and “Diversity and inclusion” (explicit values)

Our values: Integrity, Compassion, Accountability, Respect, Engagement and Excellence.

Diversity and inclusion: the organisation emphasises a diverse workforce and links this to delivering safer and more inclusive care. It encourages applicants from a wide range of backgrounds.

How to treat values in interview prep: values are behavioural standards. Your examples should sound consistent with them, even if you never say the value words out loud (which can sound a bit contrived).

What values usually look like in real answers

  • Integrity: honest limits, safe escalation, transparency, accurate documentation
  • Compassion: patient-centred language, respect, kindness under pressure
  • Accountability: follow-through, owning errors, fixing root causes
  • Respect: calm teamwork, fair treatment, inclusive communication
  • Engagement: active collaboration, not isolating yourself, contributing to the team
  • Excellence: evidence-led practice, continuous learning, improving processes

When values are not explicit

Some role descriptions do not list values clearly under headings. In those cases, you can still extract values by looking at how the role expects you to behave. Most of the time, this means considering the responsibilities and the language used. Its never a bad idea to phone the contact person to ask about what the team values in their staff, and what they think makes someone a good fit for the role.

Part 3: Implicit values and signals (these show up through responsibilities)

(Click to open) Excerpt: responsibilities that hint at implicit values and “hidden” attributes
  • Deliver high quality evidence-based clinical services within a highly functioning interprofessional team.
  • Contribute to service development, quality improvement, and clinical education.
  • Monitor and report on work practices and outcomes, including initiating, planning and evaluating relevant quality and research initiatives.
  • Apply increasingly independent clinical judgement with only occasional supervision.
  • Provide regular home visiting within a defined catchment (as directed).
  • Develop and maintain strategic links with community-based organisations to support effective handover and discharge planning.
  • Use negotiation and conflict resolution skills as required.
  • Treat patients and staff fairly and with mutual respect and understanding regardless of background.
  • Provide safe, patient-centred and compassionate care, considering psychological, social, emotional, spiritual and physical needs.
  • Prepare high quality, accurate documentation (clinical notes, recommendations, policies, reports).
  • Screen and prioritise workload demands in a complex, high patient environment.

These lines are not labelled as “values”, but they strongly suggest what kind of person the team wants. This is where you can build a more accurate attribute list than the generic criteria headings alone.

What you can see in the role description What it suggests they value
Home visiting and community handover/discharge planning Autonomy, planning, continuity of care, and safe transitions
Evidence-based practice repeatedly emphasised Clinical reasoning and up-to-date decision making
Independent judgement with occasional supervision Confidence with boundaries (knowing when to escalate)
Negotiation and conflict resolution Influence, emotional control, and professionalism
High quality written documentation Accuracy, follow-through, accountability
Fair treatment regardless of background Inclusion, cultural safety, respectful communication
Patient-centred and compassionate care (explicitly stated) This one is self-explanatory

Part 4: The “extra” desirable attributes you can add

If you read the responsibilities carefully, and consider what you think is important in the role, you can often identify extra attributes that are clearly relevant, but not explicitly listed as criteria.

Othe “extra” desirable attributes. For example, for a senior community health role such as the example:

  • Ability to cover specific areas during sick leave / annual leave
  • Ability to collect data that will assist in making the role permanent and justifying its value

How to find these extra attributes:

  • Look for responsibilities that are not fully captured by the explicit criteria.
  • Think about what you would want in the role if you were the panel.
  • Ask people who work in similar roles about what they think is important for success.

Step 2: Extract your best attributes (separately from the role)

Dont try and just match yourself to the themes you identified above. This is a common mistake and leads to vague, generic answers that do not show the panel who you really are and what you can do. Your best attributes are often what makes you memorable, unique, and what makes the panel feel confident you will do well in the team, even if it is not a perfect match. E.g. if you are a an experienced geriatric pharmacist, don't pretend you are an infectious diseases expert. Demonstrate your ability to become an expert in a new area, and how, given time, you would do the same for the new role.

2A. Start with a strengths brainstorm (by category)

Write down what your strengths are, and try to group them into themes. E.g.:

  • Clinical experience / expertise
  • Education
  • Service improvement
  • Research
  • Leadership
  • Communication
  • Organisation

2B. Choose your top ~3-5

FYI: Try to separate your ego with what your best professional attributes are. If you cannot attach good evidence to what you think is one of your strengths, it might be time to self-reflect. It can be helpful to talk with a mentor or colleague about how they see your strengths and what evidence they would point to.

What if there is a mismatch between my strengths and the identified attributes of the role?

Your best attributes might have little overlap with what you have identified the role requires. It's better to be honest with yourself about this than to try to force a match, and its better to be aware of that gap earlier than later. If this is the case, the panel may be aware prior to the interview and so you can come prepared to demonstrate how you can reconcile this. Sometimes its just not a good fit, or what you think are important skills for the role are different to what the panel thinks. Sometimes its good to miss out on jobs like this because you will probably end up feeling frustrated if it's not what you thought it would be.
