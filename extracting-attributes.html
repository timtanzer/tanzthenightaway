Extracting attributes

The fastest way to improve your interview performance is to stop preparing for “questions” and start preparing for “themes”. Public hospital interview questions often change wording from panel to panel, but the underlying themes are usually consistent.

This page is a step-by-step method to read a position description and pull out the real attributes being assessed, then turn them into a focused prep plan.

What you will produce by the end:

  • A one-sentence summary of what the role is trying to achieve.
  • A shortlist of 5 to 7 key attributes (themes) that are most likely to be assessed.
  • A simple priority ranking so you know what to practise hardest.
  • A draft map of which examples you will use to prove each attribute.
  • A list of gaps you need to address before the interview.

Note: This is not an official resource. It is a practical way of thinking about interviews based on patterns that often show up in Queensland public hospital hiring.

First, define what “attributes” means

When people say “key attributes”, they often mean a mix of:

  • Capability (what you can do reliably).
  • Behaviour (how you work with others, especially under pressure).
  • Judgement (how you think, prioritise, and manage risk).
  • Values (what you focus on when trade-offs are required).
  • Impact (what changes because you are there).

A common mistake is preparing only for tasks (what you have done) and not preparing to show the panel how you think and behave. The panel is often assessing whether they can trust your judgement, not just your technical knowledge.

Step 1: Collect your inputs

Before you highlight anything, gather the information you will be working from. Even if you only have the job ad and a short role description, that is still enough to extract a strong set of themes.

  • Position description (or job ad if that is all you have).
  • Any “about the team” paragraph (work area, service size, core activities).
  • Any mention of shift patterns or on-call (signals workload and decision-making pressure).
  • Anything about training, supervision, or supporting others (signals expectation of guidance and influence).
  • Anything about projects, improvement, or “change” (signals improvement mindset beyond day-to-day tasks).

Quick reality check: You are not trying to memorise the position description. You are extracting signals about what success in the role looks like.

Step 2: Write the role’s purpose in one sentence

This sounds too simple, but it is the anchor for everything else. If you cannot explain the purpose of the role in one sentence, your interview answers risk sounding scattered.

How to do it:

  1. Read the first paragraph and the “main duties” section.
  2. Ask: “What outcome is this role meant to create?”
  3. Write one sentence using plain language.

Template:

“This role exists to [main outcome] by [how it does it], with a strong focus on [what must not go wrong].”

Example one-sentence summaries

Example A (general clinical support): “This role exists to support safe, timely medicines care for patients by prioritising work, communicating clearly with the team, and managing risk when things change quickly.”

Example B (rotational role): “This role exists to provide dependable day-to-day medicines support across different areas by learning quickly, following safe processes, and escalating early when risk or uncertainty appears.”

Example C (more senior support role): “This role exists to improve how the medicines service functions by supporting others, strengthening safety and workflow, and contributing to service improvement that lasts beyond individual shifts.”

Keep this sentence nearby when you practise. It helps you bring your answers back to what the panel actually cares about.

Step 3: Highlight signals, not just words

Now you are ready to extract attributes. Start by scanning for:

  • Repeated ideas (if something appears 3 times, it is probably a key theme).
  • Action verbs (prioritise, coordinate, respond, review, support, escalate, educate, improve).
  • Risk language (safe, accountable, incident, governance, audit, high-risk, escalation).
  • Team language (liaise, collaborate, communicate, stakeholder, multidisciplinary).
  • Change language (project, implement, improve, review, evaluate, standardise).
  • Autonomy language (work independently, exercise judgement, minimal supervision).

When you highlight, you are not trying to build a long list. You are trying to identify the themes that will likely be assessed. Think of each highlight as a clue about what the panel is trying to confirm.

Mini example: turning phrases into signals
  • “Prioritise workload in a busy environment” often signals prioritisation, time management, calm under pressure, and safe triage.
  • “Provide advice to clinical teams” often signals communication, confidence, professional judgement, and influencing without authority.
  • “Participate in quality activities” often signals improvement mindset, attention to patterns, and willingness to fix root causes.
  • “Support training” often signals teaching ability, patience, and consistency in standards.
  • “Work collaboratively” often signals teamwork, conflict management, and respect for different roles.

Step 4: Group the signals into 5 to 7 themes

Your goal is to turn the highlighted text into a small set of themes that you can actually prepare for. If you end up with 12 themes, you will spread yourself too thin.

A good target: 5 to 7 themes, plus 1 “bonus theme” if the role is specialised.

Common themes for medicines-focused hospital roles:

  • Safety and risk management (checking, escalating, documenting, preventing harm).
  • Prioritisation and organisation (triage, competing demands, staying calm).
  • Communication (clear, respectful, accurate, tailored to the audience).
  • Teamwork and relationships (trust, reliability, conflict handling, collaboration).
  • Clinical reasoning and judgement (making sense of information, choosing actions, knowing limits).
  • Improvement mindset (small fixes, noticing patterns, making work safer or smoother).
  • Learning and adaptability (rotations, new systems, responding to feedback).

You do not need to use these headings exactly. Use words that feel natural for you. The important part is that each theme is specific enough that you can prove it with examples.

Tip: separate “communication” from “teamwork”

People often combine these, but they are different. Communication is about clarity and accuracy. Teamwork is about reliability, shared goals, and handling friction. Keeping them separate gives you more control over your examples.

Step 5: Convert themes into “proof statements”

For each theme, write a short statement that starts with “I can prove this because…”. This prevents you from staying vague.

Examples:

  • Safety and risk: “I can prove this because I have identified risks early, involved the right people, and prevented problems escalating.”
  • Prioritisation: “I can prove this because I have managed competing demands in busy periods while still completing the high-risk tasks first.”
  • Communication: “I can prove this because I have explained complex decisions in plain language and reduced confusion or delays.”
  • Improvement mindset: “I can prove this because I have noticed recurring issues and helped implement a practical fix.”

If you struggle to write a proof statement for a theme, that is a clue that you either:

  • do not have evidence yet (so you need a plan), or
  • the theme is too broad and needs tightening.

Step 6: Prioritise your themes (so you know where to spend time)

Not every theme is equally important. Prioritising helps you practise the highest-value content hardest.

A simple scoring method: score each theme 1 to 5 for the following, then add them up.

  • Frequency: how often it appears or is hinted at in the role description.
  • Risk: how serious it is if someone is weak in this area.
  • Distinctiveness: how much it differentiates strong candidates from average ones.
  • Your evidence strength: how strong your examples are right now.

Interpretation:

  • High score: build your strongest, cleanest examples here and practise them out loud.
  • Middle score: prepare at least one reliable example and one scenario approach.
  • Low score: know what you would say, but do not over-invest time.
Worksheet: theme scoring table

Copy this into your own notes and fill it out:

Theme: ________________________

Frequency (1-5): __
Risk (1-5): __
Distinctiveness (1-5): __
My evidence strength (1-5): __

Total: __ / 20

Notes on why: _______________________________________

Be honest with “evidence strength”. It is better to identify a weak area now than to get stuck in the interview with a vague answer.

Step 7: Predict likely question angles for each theme

Panels usually test a theme from different angles. If you prepare only one angle, you can feel thrown when the wording changes. For each theme, consider preparing for at least two question styles:

  • Behaviour-based: “Tell us about a time when…”
  • Scenario-based: “What would you do if…”
  • Judgement-based: “How would you prioritise…” or “How would you decide…”
  • Values-based: “What matters most to you when…”

Theme example: prioritisation

  • Behaviour: “Tell us about a time you had multiple competing tasks and how you managed it.”
  • Scenario: “What would you do if you are interrupted repeatedly while doing something high-risk?”
  • Judgement: “How do you decide what is urgent versus important?”

Step 8: Map each theme to examples (without over-preparing)

This is where you connect theme extraction to your example bank. A practical approach is:

  • Choose one primary example for each high-priority theme.
  • Choose one backup example that can also cover it if needed.
  • Make sure each example can cover more than one theme so you can adapt in the interview.

Why examples should overlap:

Real work is not neatly divided into boxes. A single strong story might demonstrate safety, communication, prioritisation, and teamwork. That is helpful because interview questions often combine themes.

Copy and fill:

- ________________________________________________

[ ] safety thinking
[ ] prioritisation under pressure
[ ] communication clarity
[ ] teamwork and influence
[ ] learning and adaptability
[ ] improvement mindset

Key outcome from my primary example (one line):
- ________________________________________________

If you cannot think of a primary example for a high-priority theme, do not panic. That simply becomes a gap to address. You can still prepare a scenario approach and show good judgement, especially if you are earlier in your career.

Step 9: “Read yourself” without making it a personality test

After you have extracted themes from the role, the next step is matching those themes to how you actually work. The aim is not to describe your personality in abstract terms. The aim is to pick a few strengths you can demonstrate clearly.

A useful rule: if you cannot support a strength with a real example, it does not belong in your shortlist.

Choose 3 to 5 strengths that you want to be remembered for.

  • Reliable under pressure
  • Calm, clear communicator
  • Safe and thorough with high-risk tasks
  • Good at building trust with the team
  • Improves processes in small practical ways
  • Teaches and supports others patiently

Step 10: Adjust your language to match the role’s scope

Different levels of roles often imply different scope. Even if the job ad does not say it directly, it can be reflected in expectations around supervision, autonomy, improvement work, and responsibility for outcomes.

A helpful way to think about scope:

  • Self: “Can I do the work safely and reliably?”
  • Team: “Can I make the team work better through communication and support?”
  • Service: “Can I improve the system, reduce recurring problems, and contribute to safer workflow?”
  • System: “Can I think beyond one area and support broader improvement and consistency?”

You do not need to pretend you operate at a broader scope than you do. Instead, show awareness. For example, you can say you escalated a pattern, contributed to a fix, or supported the team with a change.

Example: same theme, different scope

Theme: improvement mindset

  • Self focus: “I noticed my own workflow was causing delays, so I changed how I organised tasks and reduced interruptions.”
  • Team focus: “I noticed repeated confusion about a process, so I created a simple checklist and shared it with the team.”
  • Service focus: “I noticed the same issue recurring across shifts, so I raised it, helped test a change, and checked that it actually worked.”

Worked example: from a fictional role description

Below is a made-up example to show the process. The exact wording in real job ads will differ, but the extraction method stays the same.

Fictional role snippet
  • Provide safe and timely medicines support in a fast-paced environment.
  • Communicate effectively with a range of clinical staff.
  • Prioritise workload and respond to interruptions appropriately.
  • Contribute to service improvement activities and support consistent processes.
  • Support orientation and learning of newer staff members.
  • Maintain accurate documentation and follow safe procedures.

Step A: underline repeated ideas

  • Safe, timely, accurate, documentation, procedures (safety and risk)
  • Communicate effectively (communication)
  • Prioritise workload, interruptions, fast-paced (prioritisation)
  • Support learning, orientation (teaching and support)
  • Service improvement, consistent processes (improvement mindset)

Step B: convert to 5 to 7 themes

  • Safety and risk management
  • Prioritisation under pressure
  • Communication
  • Teamwork and support of others
  • Improvement mindset

Step C: write proof statements

  • “I can prove safety and risk management because…”
  • “I can prove prioritisation under pressure because…”
  • “I can prove communication because…”
  • “I can prove teamwork and support because…”
  • “I can prove improvement mindset because…”

Step D: map to examples

You would then pick an example (or two) that can cover multiple themes. For instance, an example about preventing an error could also show prioritisation, communication, and teamwork.

Common pitfalls (and how to avoid them)

  • Pitfall: listing tasks instead of impact.
    Fix: always include the outcome. What changed because of what you did?
  • Pitfall: sounding generic (“I am a hard worker”).
    Fix: use concrete evidence: what you did, what you checked, who you involved, and what improved.
  • Pitfall: over-preparing a script.
    Fix: prepare dot points, not paragraphs. You want flexible examples you can adapt.
  • Pitfall: skipping safety thinking.
    Fix: even in non-technical questions, mention risk awareness, checking, and escalation when relevant.
  • Pitfall: trying to sound senior by overreaching.
    Fix: show awareness of broader issues, but speak honestly about your role and how you influenced outcomes.
