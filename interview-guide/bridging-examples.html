Bridging examples to questions

The core skill of interviewing well is not having an example for every possible question. It is having a small bank of examples that demonstrate your best attributes, recognising what the role needs, and adapting your examples to different questions without forcing them.

What good bridging looks like

  • you start by clearly answering the question that was asked
  • you show evidence (not just opinions)
  • you make your reasoning visible
  • your example feels relevant to the question and the role
  • you finish with a clear link back to the question

What you are actually doing

  • showing you can recognise which attribute is being tested
  • showing you understand what matters in this kind of role (not just your own interests)
  • showing you can be flexible and still stay accurate
  • using the question as an opportunity to show your best qualities

Why interviews feel unpredictable

The number of possible questions feels unlimited, but most questions are just different ways of assessing the same core attributes. Panels often choose a question to test a specific competency, then score you against that competency.

So you do not need to memorise every possible question. You need to get faster at recognising what is being tested, then making it obvious in your answer.

Bridging with the five domains

On the extracting attributes page, we pulled out five domains from an example role description that often appear in public hospital medicines roles:

  • Clinical (safe decisions, evidence, judgement, managing risk)
  • Communication and teamwork (influence, conflict, collaboration, clarity)
  • Working independently (organisation, prioritisation, autonomy, follow-through)
  • Leadership (supporting others, initiative, leading without authority)
  • Service improvement (quality, evaluation, practical change, learning loops)
The questions below are paraphrased examples of what a panel might ask. They are here to help you practise recognising the domain and choosing the right angle.

Our worked example: clozapine and QT project

We will use one base story and practise bridging it across the five domains. The facts will stay the same, but what changes is what you emphasise.

Click to open: the base story (short version)

Base story summary

  • Situation: In an early-career rotational role I learnt more about ECG interpretation through teaching and ward exposure. When I later worked in a mental health setting, I noticed frequent “prolonged QT” flags in people taking clozapine and other antipsychotics.
  • Problem: QT flags can drive high-stakes decisions. I was concerned common measurement and correction methods might inflate QTc readings at higher heart rates, pushing teams towards unhelpful or inconsistent decisions.
  • Actions: I checked the evidence, sanity-checked my understanding with more experienced clinicians, reviewed cases to confirm the pattern, ran a pilot project.
  • Outcome: The pilot supported that the method used can dramatically change who is labelled “at risk”. It gave a rationale for a better, larger study, and changed how I personally responded to QT flags in day-to-day practice.

Quick rules for bridging

  • Keep: the core facts, your role, and the outcomes - you want to get across the 'your best attributes' part of the examples - thats why you chose this example
  • Shift: what you spend time on
  • Cut: details that do not score for the domain you are answering
  • Add: things that makes the tested attribute obvious

What not to do

  • do not pretend you had authority you did not have
  • do not invent details
  • do not force the story if it does not fit the question

Domain 1: Clinical

Some interviews will assess clinical expertise through clinical cases. However, others may instead ask you to discuss a clinical scenario in a similar fashion to a behavioural question. In more senior roles, it becomes more common to ask these questions as they demonstrate higher level thinking better, whereas junior roles are more likely to have clinical cases to assess if you are safe for practice. Clinical questions are usually testing safe judgement, evidence-led thinking, risk awareness, whether you have the required competency and knowledge for the role, and whether you know your limits and escalate appropriately.

Example questions

  1. Tell us about a time you noticed a risk to patient safety. How did you spot it and what did you do?
  2. Describe a time you had to make a recommendation with incomplete or conflicting information.
  3. Tell us about a time you used evidence from multiple sources to guide a clinical decision.
  4. Describe a time you had to seek help for a clinical recommendation. Why, and how did you do it?
  5. Tell us about a time you had to defend a clinical recommendation when others were unsure or disagreed.
  6. Describe a decision you later adjusted after new information emerged. What did you learn?

Bridging the QT example for clinical scenarios

To bridge the QT example to a clinical question like this, I want to emphasise the clinical impact. So the story might best start with a case on the wards. After explaining the case situation, I can give the context about my prior knowledge of the QT issue, and how I noticed an issue. I would then explain the bulk of the QT project story, talking about the project, but emphasising the clinical impact of this project, and minimising other unneccessary details. At the end, I would return to the case scenario, discussing the outcome for the patient in the original case, and discussing my new, updated approach to dealing with these cases. I now have a great clinical story with a good patient outcome, where expert clinical knowledge was demonstrated, and importantly - I was still was able to tell them about this awesome research project I did and get my best attributes across. Now, instead of just telling them about a great intervention, the 'context' of why the intervention was important, is this great project.

Domain 2: Communication and teamwork

These questions are usually assessing whether you can work well with others, communicate clearly under pressure, adapt your style, and handle disagreement without becoming vague or defensive.

Example questions

  1. Tell us about a time you encountered conflict or disagreement in the workplace. How did you respond?
  2. Describe a sensitive or awkward conversation you have had at work. How did you handle it?
  3. Tell us about a time someone disagreed with your recommendation. What did you do?
  4. Give an example of a time you changed your communication style for a different person or situation.
  5. Describe a time you had to gather information by asking multiple people questions.
  6. Tell us about a time you helped a team succeed by sharing knowledge or improving team decisions.

Communication and teamwork bridging summary (what to emphasise)

When the question is communication or teamwork, a project example might seem a far stretch, but depending on the other questions in the interview, it might be the only opportunity to give the example and get your best attributes across. We can still make it work with the research project as the backdrop. What we should focus on is whether your behaviour during conflict makes work clearer, calmer, and safer for other people. In the clinical-focused question, we began the 'story' with a clinical case on the wards where there was a prolonged QT. this situation, we could start the story regarding a disagreement between the treating team and myself about the QT risk of a particular patient. In this scenario, a patient was flagged as having a prolonged QT, and the psychiatry team referred to cardiology, who instructed to cease the antipsychotic. This was unnecessary as the QT was not actually prolonged when you took the heart rate into account, and stopping the antipsychotic would have put the patient at risk of psychosis relapse (this really happened). So the 'conflict' here is not a personal disagreement, but rather a pharmacist and a cardiologist having a professional disagreement. The focus of the example instead, is on the approach to dealing with the conflict - e.g., speaking to the cardiology team directly, asking questions to understand their concerns, what to do if they continue to disagree. In this example, when it comes to time to 'explain' why I was correct - I can then give the QT project example as background, and still get across this awesome project I designed while answering a question about communication. A nice conclusion in the 'reflections' part of this answer is getting guidelines changed - such as getting the metro south guidelines changed to advise on the use of the QT nomogram and against the use of the Bazetts formula for this purpose (more on this in the next page). Although project examples like this dont usually come to mind for communication questions, they can be quite good because you are systemically resolving conflicts. Always try to avoid sounding like you are 'right', and instead focus on the shared goal of good outcomes for the patient. A 'nice' way to resolve a professional conflict with a consultant cardiologist is to get the work published and the guidelines updated.

Domain 3: Working independently

These questions are usually about prioritisation, time management, autonomy, follow-through, and reliable work standards. They are also about how you manage obstacles without becoming chaotic or needing constant rescue.

Example questions

  1. Describe a time you had several competing priorities. How did you decide what came first?
  2. Your plan hit a major obstacle. What did you do to keep progress moving?
  3. Describe a time you worked with minimal supervision. How did you stay safe and effective?
  4. What systems do you use to prevent errors and maintain accuracy?
  5. Tell us about a time you delivered high-quality work despite interruptions or time pressure.
  6. Describe a time you felt under pressure at work. How did you manage yourself?

Working independently bridging summary (what to emphasise)

To bridge an example like the QT project to a question about working independently, I want to emphasise how I organised the project work and kept it feasible, and how I adapted when I hit obstacles. Its also important to talk about prioritisation - e.g. how did I manage my workload so I could do this project alongside clinical work without it interfering with patient care. I can talk about how the project affects prioritsation in clinical review. For example, I can talk about how I used to get a prolonged QT flag and just panic and refer to cardiology, but now I have a more structured approach to prioritising the information I need to review before making a decision. This one is a bit more of a stretch than the clinical and communication questions, but it is still a good opportunity to get this project story in and show your best attributes around organisation, adaptability, and quality control. Behaviours that can be necessary to complete a large research project (teamwork, asking for help, etc) are also applicable to clinical work. This project was also on a topic with no 'experts' or authority on the subject, meaning it can be used to answer questions like 'what did you do when you had to make a decision and your supervisor was away'.

  • Show a method, not a mood: explain how you organised your thinking and your time, not just that you were “busy”.
  • Highlight feasibility: “I kept it small enough to deliver alongside clinical workload.”
  • Demonstrate quality control: consistency of method, checks for accuracy, clear documentation, and knowing when to ask for input.
  • Make the pivot a strength: systems obstacles happen, your value is that you can adapt and still produce something meaningful.
  • Stay humble about autonomy: independent does not mean solo. It means ownership plus sensible escalation points.

Domain 4: Leadership

Leadership questions can assess several different skills. They often assess initiative, influence without authority, supporting others, and whether you can lift the standard around you. Higher roles may assess more formal leadership, which could be more considered 'management'.

Example questions

  1. Tell us about a time you took the lead on something important without being asked.
  2. Describe a time you supported a less experienced colleague or student to do quality work.
  3. Give an example of influencing others when you did not have authority.
  4. Tell us about a time you met resistance to an idea. How did you respond?
  5. Describe a time you brought people together to achieve an objective.
  6. Describe a time you improved standards by being clear about expectations and quality.

Leadership bridging summary (what to emphasise)

Leadership at early to mid-level is mostly about behaviour, initiative, influence and supporting others without formal authority. For the QT story, the leadership angle is that I took ownership of a problem with real clinical consequences and you did something about it. E.g. testing it in a practical way, involving the right people, mentoring students, junior doctors, pharmacists, managing the project and getting it published, and then getting the guidelines updated. I can also talk about how I handled resistance to the project, and how I built support for it by making it easy for others to engage with it (e.g. how I pitched it to more senior clinicians to make them care about it and get on board, how I made it easy for others to contribute, how I made the work safe to engage with). As an example, I could describe a specific instance where I identified a key stakeholder who was initially resistant to the project and worked to understand their concerns, ultimately bringing them on board by addressing their needs and demonstrating the value of the project. I could also talk about how I mentored a junior doctor or student to get involved in the project, teaching them the methods and checking their work, which not only helped the project but also developed their skills and confidence. All of these things are true, but they are not usually the things that come to mind when you think of an example involving a research project, so you have to be intentional about highlighting them when answering leadership questions. Ultimately leading this project shows that I can take initiative, build support, mentor others, meet deadlines, resolve conflicts, and raise standards around me - all of which are important leadership attributes that I want to get across in the interview.

  • Leadership starts when you move from noticing to doing: you took ownership of testing an issue, not just talking about it.
  • Make “how” visible: you scoped it, sought appropriate supervision, involved the right people, and chose a feasible first step.
  • Show leadership through support: mentoring a student, setting quality expectations, and checking work so others can learn and the output is trustworthy.
  • Demonstrate psychological safety: your approach invited people in rather than putting them on the defensive.
  • Give credit and avoid hero language: leadership sounds credible when it is collaborative and proportionate to your level.

A common trap is over-claiming responsibility. You can still show leadership without being overconfident, while being honest about what you did, and how you relied on others.

Domain 5: Service / quality improvement

Service or quality improvement questions are testing whether you can notice problems worth fixing, choose a feasible method, measure something meaningful, and follow through. They also test whether you understand the quality improvement cycle.

Example questions

  1. Tell us about a time you identified a recurring process problem and what you did about it.
  2. Describe a quality activity you were involved in. What was your role and what changed?
  3. Tell us about a time you used evidence or data to guide practice.
  4. Describe a time you trialled a new approach and checked whether it was effective.
  5. Tell us about a mistake or near-miss that changed how you work.
  6. Describe a new idea you suggested and how you got others on board.

Service improvement bridging summary (what to emphasise)

When the question is improvement, The QT project obviously fits the neatest in its base form given it is a research project. In this scenario, you would focus on the details and outcomes of the project more, however you still want to communicate key behaviours. Make the example about the system. Not just 'I learned about QT prolongation and taught other people about it', but 'we had a recurring process issue that affected decision quality, and I took ownership of designing a project to test a practical solution'. Its important to define what 'better' looks like in the context of the project. For example, better could be more accurate classification of QT risk, calmer decisions about whether to stop or start medications, fewer unnecessary escalations to cardiology, clearer documentation of QT risk, and safer continuity of care for patients with QT risk.

What if they just ask: “Why are you the best person for the role?”

This question feels broad and can be tricky to answer because it invites you to ramble. The easiest way to stay structured is to use the work you already did in the extracting attributes step.

A strong answer does three things: (1) shows you understand what the role needs, (2) gives evidence you meet those needs, (3) shows you have a plan for how you will deliver in the role.

A simple structure you can rehearse

Start by showing role insight. This is where you demonstrate that you have thought about what success looks like in this role, not just what you personally enjoy doing. A simple way to say it is: “From reading the role description and understanding the service, I think success in this role depends on five things.”

  • broad clinical judgement and safe decision making
  • strong communication and teamwork
  • the ability to work independently and prioritise in complexity
  • leadership (including supporting others and lifting standards)
  • a track record of service improvement and follow-through

Then, instead of listing five mini bullet proofs, speak in connected prose. You are trying to sound like a real person who understands the role, not someone reciting a checklist. You can still cover all five domains, just do it in a way that flows.

For example, you might say that your clinical strength is evidence-led judgement and risk management. Your communication strength is being able to take complicated information, translate it into a clear risk message, and align a team around a consistent plan. Your independent work strength is staying structured under pressure and following things through, including when the ideal path is blocked and you have to adapt. Your leadership strength is taking initiative in a level-appropriate way, involving the right people early, supporting others, and lifting standards without needing authority. And your improvement strength is being able to turn a recurring issue into a practical, measurable pilot and then feed the learning back into everyday practice.

If you have one example that genuinely represents your best work, it is fine to reference it briefly as you talk. Keep it to one or two sentences each time. You are proving the claim, not retelling a full story.

Why a 12 month plan matters

This question is not only about whether you can interview well. It is also about whether the panel can picture you in the role and trust you to deliver. A short 12 month plan reduces perceived risk. It shows you understand how to get up to speed, how you prioritise, and how you will turn your skills into real outcomes for the team. It also signals that you will not wait passively for direction, but you will still work within the local priorities and governance.

Your plan does not need to be grand. It should sound realistic and grounded in how public hospital roles actually work: learn the local systems first, become reliably independent, then improve something that matters.

A simple 12 month plan you can adapt

  • First 4 weeks: learn the local workflow, meet key stakeholders, understand the service priorities, and identify quick wins that improve safety and reduce friction for the team.
  • By 3 months: be reliably independent in the core workload, contribute consistently in multidisciplinary discussions, and identify one improvement opportunity worth scoping properly.
  • By 6 months: deliver one concrete improvement activity (pilot, audit, or small change) with clear documentation and feedback to the team.
  • By 12 months: have at least one measurable improvement outcome, evidence of supporting others (students or newer staff), and a plan for the next improvement cycle.
