Building examples

Once you have pulled out the likely attributes of the role, the next step is building a set of examples that you can actually use in the interview. This is where a lot of people either over-prepare, under-prepare, or prepare in the wrong way.

The goal is not to write perfect scripts for every possible question. The goal is to build a small bank of strong, flexible examples that show what you can do, how you think, and what kind of person you are to work with.

What you are trying to build

  • a small bank of strong examples
  • stories that prove more than one attribute
  • examples you can shorten or expand depending on the question
  • examples that show both what you did and how you thought

What you are not trying to build

  • a script for every likely question
  • 20 examples you can barely remember
  • stories that are all technically impressive but say nothing about your judgement
  • generic answers that could belong to anyone
Good interview examples are portable. One strong example should often be usable for more than one attribute or question theme.

Start with the overlap, not with the script

You should already have two separate lists from the previous page:

  • the attributes you think the role is likely to care about
  • your own strongest attributes

Your best examples usually come from the overlap between those two lists. That is where you are most likely to sound confident, natural, and convincing.

Best place to start

  • attributes the role seems to care about
  • attributes you genuinely do well
  • situations where you can clearly show your decision making
  • stories with a real outcome, not just activity

Then cover the gaps

  • pick one or two examples that help cover obvious weak spots
  • these do not need to be your most impressive stories
  • they do need to show safe thinking, good judgement, and self-awareness

For example, if one of your best strengths is education but the role clearly also values prioritisation, documentation, and communication across teams, then your example bank needs to show those areas too. It does not mean pretending they are your strongest features. It means making sure you are not silent on them.

What makes a good interview example?

Not every work story is worth taking into an interview. A strong example usually has some combination of pressure, complexity, judgement, communication, or change.

Usually strong examples

  • something important was at stake
  • there was some complexity, uncertainty, or pressure
  • you had to decide what mattered most
  • your actions made a clear difference
  • there was a visible outcome or learning point

Usually weak examples

  • it was just routine work with no real judgement involved
  • the story is mostly about what the team did, not what you did
  • the outcome is vague or unclear
  • there is no real challenge in the story
  • you cannot explain why you chose one action over another

A lot of candidates assume their best examples are the biggest or most impressive things they have ever done. Often that is not true. Panels usually learn more from examples where your judgement is clear than from examples that just sound grand.

A small but well-explained example is often better than a huge project that you cannot describe clearly.

The six ingredients of a strong example

You do not need a fancy acronym, but you do need the right ingredients. Most strong examples include the following:

Ingredient What it means Why it matters
Context What was happening, where you were working, and what the situation was. Gives enough background for the panel to follow the story.
Challenge What made the situation difficult, risky, sensitive, busy, or unclear. Shows why the example is worth telling in the first place.
Your goal What you were trying to achieve in that moment. Shows that your actions were purposeful, not random.
Your actions What you actually did, in a clear order. This is the core of the answer and where most of the marks sit.
Your judgement What you were considering, balancing, checking, or escalating. This shows how you think, not just what you did.
Outcome and reflection What changed, what was learned, and what happened afterwards. Shows impact, learning, and whether you improve over time.

Most weak answers spend too long on the background and not enough time on actions, judgement, and outcome. If you are telling an example and you are still setting the scene after 30 seconds, that is usually a warning sign.

A useful rule of thumb: keep the background brief, then spend most of your time on what you did, why you did it, and what happened because of it.

Build a small core bank, not a giant library

A good example bank is usually much smaller than people expect. For most interviews, you do not need 20 polished stories. You need a handful of good ones that can flex.

A practical target is usually 6 to 8 core examples. If you are earlier in your career, even 5 very good examples can be enough if they are well chosen.

Type of example Why it earns its place What it can often cover
Prioritising under pressure Very commonly assessed and shows judgement quickly. Prioritisation, risk, communication, organisation, calmness
Difficult communication or conflict Shows maturity, influence, and professionalism. Communication, teamwork, respect, negotiation, leadership
Identifying and managing risk Shows safety thinking and reliability. Judgement, accountability, clinical reasoning, documentation
Improving a process or fixing a recurring issue Shows initiative and follow-through. Improvement, leadership, service thinking, evidence-based practice
Supporting, supervising, or teaching someone Shows patience, standards, and ability to lift others. Leadership, communication, teamwork, education
Working across teams, services, or settings Shows handover, collaboration, and broader awareness. Communication, coordination, patient-centred care, planning
Adapting to change or uncertainty Shows flexibility and resilience without becoming fluffy. Learning, initiative, prioritisation, judgement
A setback, mistake, or near miss that led to learning Shows honesty, reflection, and accountability. Integrity, safety, learning, maturity, improvement

Not everyone will have all of these. That is fine. The point is to think in categories, not to force a perfect set.

One example should cover more than one thing

This is one of the most important ideas in the whole guide. Your examples do not need to live in neat boxes. In fact, the most useful examples usually prove several attributes at once.

Example

A story about managing a complex discharge or community handover might also show:

  • prioritisation
  • communication
  • documentation
  • patient-centred care
  • working across boundaries

Why this matters

If a story only works for one very narrow question, it is not very efficient. Good examples are reusable. They just need to be told with a different emphasis depending on what the panel asks.

When building your example bank, ask yourself: what else could this story prove?

Build each story as an “example card”

Do not write full scripts. Build each example as a short card in your notes, using dot points only. You want enough structure to remember it, but not so much that you sound rehearsed.

What to include on an example card

  • a short title you will remember quickly
  • the situation in one or two lines
  • what made it difficult
  • what you were trying to achieve
  • what you actually did
  • what you were thinking or balancing
  • the outcome
  • what it says about you
  • other themes the same story could cover

Why this format works

  • it is easier to revise quickly
  • it is easier to adapt on the day
  • it helps you avoid memorised speeches
  • it makes it easier to shorten or expand a story

Example title: give it a short name that makes sense to you.

  • Situation: where were you, and what was happening?
  • Challenge: what made it difficult, risky, busy, or sensitive?
  • Goal: what were you trying to achieve?
  • Actions: what did you do, in order?
  • Judgement: what were you considering, checking, escalating, or balancing?
  • Outcome: what changed, improved, or was prevented?
  • Reflection: what did you learn or do differently afterwards?
  • Other themes: what else could this story be used for?

Build short and long versions of the same story

A useful example should exist in more than one length. Sometimes the panel asks a very specific question and you only need a tight answer. Other times the question is broad and you need a fuller version.

Short version

  • about 45 to 60 seconds
  • minimal background
  • one clear challenge
  • your key actions
  • clear outcome

Full version

  • about 90 seconds to 2 minutes
  • slightly more context
  • clearer explanation of your reasoning
  • more nuance in the actions
  • reflection or learning point
If an example only works when told as a long story, it probably needs tightening. Interview answers usually improve when they become clearer, not longer.

Worked example: one story, several uses

Here is a fictional example built around the kind of work suggested by the case-study role. It is not meant to be copied word for word. It is there to show what a flexible, high-value example looks like.

Click to open: fictional example card

Example title: Home discharge, medicine changes, unclear handover

  • Situation: an older patient was moving through a home-based model of care with multiple recent medicine changes, and the information held by different team members was not fully consistent.
  • Challenge: there was a real risk of confusion after discharge, the patient’s support person was unsure what had changed, and there were several other competing clinical demands at the same time.
  • Goal: make the medicine plan clear, reduce immediate risk, and ensure the next team received accurate information.
  • Actions: reviewed the available information, clarified the intended plan with the relevant team members, prioritised the issue because of its risk, explained the changes in plain language, documented the final plan clearly, and made sure the handover was completed properly.
  • Judgement: recognised that the biggest risk was inconsistency across settings, so focused first on alignment, communication, and documentation rather than trying to do everything at once.
  • Outcome: the patient and support person left with a clear plan, the receiving team had consistent documentation, and the case highlighted a handover issue that could be improved more broadly.
  • What this could prove: prioritisation, communication, risk management, documentation, patient-centred care, coordination across settings, and service improvement thinking.

This is a strong example because it is not trapped inside one theme. Depending on the question, you could tell this as a story about prioritisation, communication, patient-centred care, documentation, or safe handover.

“We were very busy and I always work hard.” “There were competing demands, so I triaged the highest-risk issue first and explained my reasoning clearly.”

Common mistakes when building examples

  • Writing full scripts. This often makes people sound stiff and harder to follow.
  • Choosing routine examples. If nothing difficult happened, the panel learns very little.
  • Talking mostly about the team. Teamwork matters, but the panel still needs to know what you did.
  • Leaving out the outcome. If the story has no result, it feels unfinished.
  • Leaving out the reasoning. The panel often wants to know how you thought, not just what you did.
  • Building too many examples. A smaller set of better examples is usually stronger.
  • Choosing examples that only prove one thing. Flexible examples are much more useful.
By the end of this step, you should feel that you have a manageable bank of examples you could actually talk through under pressure. Not perfect. Just strong, flexible, and real.
