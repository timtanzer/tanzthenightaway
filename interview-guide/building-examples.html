Building examples

Once you have pulled out the likely attributes of the role, the next step is building a set of examples that you can use in the interview.

The goal is not to write perfect scripts for every possible question. The goal is to build a small bank of strong, flexible examples that show what you can do, how you think, and what kind of person you are to work with.

What you are trying to build

  • a small bank of strong examples
  • examples you can shorten or expand depending on the question
  • examples that show both what you did and how you thought
  • examples where your role is clear

You do not need to have

  • a script for every possible question
  • 20 examples you can barely remember
  • stories that are all technically impressive but say nothing about your judgement
  • generic or shallow examples that could belong to anyone
Later (on the bridging page), we will look at how one example can be emphasised differently depending on the question. For now, this page is about building the examples themselves.

What makes a good interview example?

You should already have two separate lists from the previous page:

  • the attributes you think the panel is likely to care about
  • your own strongest attributes

Your best examples usually come from the overlap between those two lists. That is where you are most likely to sound confident, natural, and convincing.

A strong example usually has some combination of pressure, complexity, judgement, communication, or change.

Usually strong examples

  • something important was at stake
  • there was complexity, uncertainty, or pressure
  • you had to decide what mattered most
  • your actions made a clear difference
  • there was a visible outcome or learning point

Usually weak examples

  • routine work with no judgement involved
  • the story is mostly about what the team did, not what you did
  • the outcome is vague or unclear
  • there is no real challenge in the story
  • you cannot explain why you chose one action over another
A lot of candidates assume their best examples are the biggest or most impressive things they have ever done. Often that is not true. Panels usually learn more from examples where your judgement is clear than from examples that just sound grand. A small but well-explained example is often better than a huge project that you cannot describe clearly, or where your role was not clear.

Most strong examples include the following:

Ingredient What it means Why it matters
Context What was happening, where you were working, and what the situation was. Gives enough background for the panel to follow the story. Explains why your actions were important.
Challenge What made the situation difficult, risky, sensitive, busy, or unclear. Shows why the example is worth telling in the first place.
Your goal What you were trying to achieve in that moment. Shows that your actions were purposeful, not random.
Your actions What you actually did, in a clear order. This is the core of the answer.
Your judgement What you were considering, balancing, checking, or escalating. This shows how you think, not just what you did.
Outcome and reflection What changed, what was learned, and what happened afterwards. Shows impact, learning, and whether you improve over time.

Note that the above does not need to be followed chronologically. For example, you might stop at several points during 'your actions' to explain the 'why' behind your decisions (judgement) or the 'why it was important' (context). The key is that all of those ingredients are present in some way, and that the panel can follow the logic of the story clearly. SBAR is a useful framework for getting the scenario down clearly, but it is easy to spend too much time on background. You can use SBAR first to get the story straight, then re-shape it to ensure it has the elements above. Weak answers often spend too little time on actions, judgement, and outcome. If it takes you a long time to set the scene, you probably need to tighten the example.

Build a small core bank of examples

A good example bank is usually much smaller than people expect. For most interviews, you do not need 10+ polished stories. You need a handful of good ones that you can recall under pressure.

A practical target is usually around 6 core examples. If you are earlier in your career, 4 good examples can be enough if they are well chosen. Your first challenge is to find a set of examples that demonstrate your best attributes that you identified in the previous step.

Types of common examples in junior to mid-level health practitioner roles
Prioritising under pressure
Difficult communication or conflict
Identifying and managing risk
Improving a process or fixing a recurring issue (think about projects or research through this lens)
Supporting, supervising, or teaching someone
Working across teams, services, or settings
Adapting to change or uncertainty
A setback, mistake, or near miss that led to learning

More senior roles still come back to these categories. The difference is usually scale and responsibility, which we will cover later.

Build each story as an “example card”

You can write a full script the first time you put together an example, to make sure you capture the details but you do not want to rehearse a full script for every example. Build each example as a short card in your notes using dot points only that you can follow on the day. You want enough structure to remember it, but not so much that you sound over-rehearsed or appear to be reading from a page or miss what the question is actually asking.

What to include on an example card

  • a short title you will remember quickly
  • the situation in one or two lines
  • what made it difficult
  • what you were trying to achieve
  • what you actually did
  • what you were thinking or balancing
  • the outcome
  • what it says about you

Why this format works

  • it is easier to revise quickly
  • it is easier to adapt on the day
  • it helps you avoid memorised speeches
  • it makes it easier to shorten or expand a story
Click to open: example card format
  • Situation: where were you, and what was happening?
  • Challenge: what made it difficult, risky, busy, or sensitive?
  • Goal: what were you trying to achieve?
  • Actions: what did you do, in order?
  • Judgement: what were you considering, checking, escalating, or balancing?
  • Outcome: what changed, improved, or was prevented?
  • Reflection: what did you learn or do differently afterwards?
Practise giving your examples in different lengths. Sometimes you will have plenty of time and sometimes you will not. Interview examples are better when they are clearer, not longer, so only lengthen them if it makes the logic easier to follow. Have a 5 minute version and a 10 minute version.

Worked example: turning a research idea into an example card

Below is an example of my “QT project” story, framed as an early-career rotational clinician (which I used often at the time). It is an abridged version of the basic facts. The point here is not the technical detail, but showing how I noticed a genuine problem, and did something about it.

Click to open: clozapine and QT project (early-career version)
  • Situation: In a rotational role I spent time in an emergency setting where I attended toxicology teaching and joined ward rounds. I learnt how QT intervals are measured and how ECG reports are interpreted. When I later rotated back into a mental health setting, I noticed frequent “prolonged QT” flags in people taking clozapine and other antipsychotics.
  • Challenge: The clinical response to a flagged prolonged QT can be high-stakes and causes anxiety, avoidable referrals, and sometimes pressure to change or stop effective treatment. I also realised there were two common technical pitfalls that could inflate QTc readings, especially when the heart rate is high, which is common with several antipsychotics.
  • Goal: Work out whether we were over-estimating QT risk in this patient group, and if so, build a sensible, evidence-led way to assess risk more accurately.
  • Actions:
    • Did background research with toxicologist to understand what QTc formulas the ECG machines use, when they become unreliable, and what the evidence says about clinical risk.
    • Had a toxicologist teach me to read ECGs and go through case examples in ED.
    • Went back through cases in the mental health setting (my next rotation) and documented the pattern I was seeing which was frequent “prolonged QTc” flags in tachycardic patients on antipsychotics.
    • With the help of my clinical educator, conducted a small QUM project as a pilot, then used the results to develop a year-long pharmacy majors project and determined what data we needed, what we would compare, and what an acceptable output would look like (something that could actually change practice).
    • We manually measured the ECGs of 1,000 patients on antipsychotics, compared of machine-reported QTc versus manual QT and alternative correction methods, and a basic summary of how many patients would be flagged depending on the method used.
  • Judgement: If QT risk was being overestimated, it could have real negative consequences for patients, including unnecessary cessation of last-resort medicines, rehospitalisation and life-altering consequences for people with schizophrenia. My first reaction was curiosity, but I also knew I needed to be careful not to jump to conclusions based on a few cases. I did not want to overstate a problem just because I had noticed a pattern. So I approached it in stages. First, I tried to understand the issue properly by learning how the QT was being measured, what the common correction formulas were doing, and where the limitations were. Then I checked my understanding with people who had more expertise than I did, rather than assuming I was right. Once I felt confident there was a genuine issue, I started thinking about how to test it in a way that was realistic. I was also very conscious of scope. As an early-career rotational clinician, my role was not to completely redesign practice on my own. My role was to define the problem clearly, gather evidence, involve the right people, and take a sensible first step.
  • Outcome: We found the Bazett's formula greatly overestimated the risk of QT prolongation in people on antipsychotics (230 vs 6 patients) compared with manual measurement and the QT nomogram. The study confirmed that the method used to flag QTc can dramatically change how many people appear “at risk”. It gave us a clear rationale for a larger, better designed study, and it also changed how I personally responded to QT flags in clinical setting.
  • Reflection: What I learnt from this was that good quality improvement / service improvement / etc often starts with noticing something that feels wrong, but then being disciplined about how you respond to it. I tried to stay evidence-led, keep checking my assumptions, and stay honest about what I could and could not show from the data we had. It also changed how I behaved clinically. After that, I became much less likely to react automatically to a flagged QTc and much more likely to stop, check the context, think about heart rate and the correction method being used, and consider what the result actually meant for that patient. I considered that often pharmacists place interventions based on QTc flags without checking the context, and that this can lead to unnecessary anxiety and referrals. I also started to have more confidence in my own ability to interpret ECGs and understand the limitations of the tools we use, which was a big step for me as a junior clinician. So the project changed how I approached a recurring clinical problem in practice.

Common mistakes when building examples

  • Following a full script. This often makes people sound stiff and harder to follow.
  • Choosing routine examples. If nothing difficult happened, the panel learns very little about you.
  • Talking mostly about the team. Teamwork matters, but the panel still needs to know what you did.
  • Leaving out the outcome. If the story has no result or is not completed in some way, it feels unfinished.
  • Leaving out the reasoning. The panel often wants to know how you thought, not just what you did.
By the end of this step, you should feel that you have a manageable bank of examples that show off your best qualities.
